Some time ago, I decided to completely rewrite my portfolio. I changed technologies, reorganised components, improved the architecture, and did what every developer eventually does: found perfectly justifiable reasons to rebuild something that was already working.

I've written about that before. But there was one thing that remained virtually untouched throughout the entire process: the visual identity.

My website was green.

There was nothing particularly wrong with that. Green worked, it looked pleasant, it suited the interface, and it served its purpose reasonably well. The problem was that, when I started thinking about why I'd chosen that colour, I realised something rather curious.

I had no reason whatsoever.

Green just happened.

And that started to bother me.

## R — Rupture

For much of my life as a developer, I saw my portfolio as an extension of my work. A place to showcase projects, share knowledge, write a few things, and occasionally convince someone that I know what I'm doing.

It was a perfectly reasonable approach.

But the more I immersed myself in music, literature, philosophy, and my own personal projects, the more I realised that my identity couldn't simply be reduced to the technologies I use professionally. Something along the lines of, "Hey, I know what I'm doing, and I don't need to keep convincing people of that."

In my view, getting a job is almost a game of "I'll pretend I know what I'm doing" versus "I'll pretend I believe you". Because with every new project, every new company, every new service, every new... well, anything, we have to learn all over again, readjust, and even allow ourselves to try something new. I might be a senior developer in X but not in Y, and that's perfectly fine. I'm not Claude.

I like Go, Rust, Solidity, and software engineering. I enjoy studying compilers, distributed systems, and things that would probably make a normal person reconsider their life choices.

But I also love music, science fiction, history, and questioning the importance humanity assigns to itself in a universe that seems utterly indifferent to our existence.

All of that is part of who I am.

It was from this realisation that my digital identity as **antianthropic** was born — or, more accurately, consolidated.

It's not about rejecting humanity, but rather questioning anthropocentrism. I don't believe we're at the centre of anything, nor that the universe owes us any particular purpose. Even so, I recognise that there's something extraordinary about our ability to create, understand, feel, and give meaning to things.

There is beauty in chaos, even when there is no purpose.

And if I was willing to build an identity that represented these ideas, why would I continue using a colour that had never meant absolutely anything to me?

Green had to go.

Not because it was ugly, inappropriate, or outdated.

But because it was accidental.

## E — Essence

Curiously enough, the answer wasn't to be found in any design trend, component library, or colour combination suggested by some tool.

It was in crimson.

Technically, all I did was take another look at my family's coat of arms, and there it was: a deep, dark red, leaning ever so slightly towards magenta. Unlike pure red, it doesn't convey just immediate intensity. There's something more restrained, denser, and, to my eyes, more elegant about it.

But its significance goes far beyond appearance.

Crimson became the colour I chose to represent my family.

We're complicated people. Temperamental, intense, occasionally irritable, and certainly a long way from any ideal of perfection. We have our contradictions, our conflicts, and our peculiarities.

And I like that honesty.

I have no interest in constructing a family symbolism that represents nothing but imaginary virtues, nonexistent glories, or some supposed hereditary superiority.

Crimson represents precisely what we are: our intensity, our imperfections, our blood, and the continuity we hope to build.

It also represents courage, perseverance, and responsibility. After all, the same temperament that can give rise to conflict can also fuel the determination to protect, build, and persevere.

In various heraldic traditions, red is associated with courage and strength. Crimson, meanwhile, has a long historical association with prestigious fabrics, distinction, and power.

I appreciate those associations, but I have no intention of claiming a nobility I don't possess (unfortunately).

My idea of distinction has far more to do with personal responsibility than with a privilege inherited at birth.

This whole chaotic collection of things that intertwine and seem to make no sense is what represents my family, and probably what makes us who we are, each in our own way.

They're references I've encountered throughout my life which, little by little, have contributed to an expression of my own.

The aim was never to look like something else.

It was to find something that looked like me.

## D — Direction

After choosing crimson, a rather practical question arose: how do you turn a colour laden with personal meaning into a visual identity that also works professionally?

Because, as much as I love red, turning my entire website into one enormous crimson surface probably wouldn't be a particularly clever decision.

The solution was restraint.

Crimson became the primary colour, accompanied by shades of deep burgundy, charcoal, ivory, and subtle touches of bronze.

The result is a predominantly dark palette, where red doesn't have to compete for attention with dozens of equally eye-catching elements.

It appears where it actually matters.

In highlights, interactions, details, and the elements that establish the website's personality.

The intention wasn't to create an extravagant interface. Quite the opposite.

I've always liked the idea that elegance is closely related to the ability to remove what isn't necessary.

I wanted to preserve the clarity of a software engineering portfolio whilst giving it a visual language that didn't look as though it had come straight out of a generic template.

And, above all, I wanted this identity to accompany me throughout my career without depending on a particular technology, job, or phase of my professional life.

Today, I work in software development and blockchain. Tomorrow, I might be working with technologies that don't even exist yet.

My tools will certainly change.

My way of thinking will continue to evolve as well.

But that doesn't mean my identity needs to be rebuilt from scratch every time something changes.

That's precisely the difference between choosing a colour because it's fashionable and choosing one because it actually means something.

The former may grow old alongside the trend.

The latter may acquire even more meaning over time.

## It's not red, it's crimson

If someone visits my website today, they'll probably see nothing more than a dark interface with crimson accents.

And that's perfectly fine.

I don't expect every visitor to understand the symbolism behind a CSS variable. That would be a rather absurd expectation, especially considering that most people probably just want to find my projects or work out whether I can write reasonably decent code.

But I know why that colour is there.

I know why it appears in my portfolio, in my digital identity, and in the visual elements associated with my family.

And that's enough.

Green was a choice that happened to me.

**Crimson is a choice I made consciously.**

Perhaps that's the real reason behind this change.

It's not about abandoning one colour for another, nor about turning a personal website into an exaggerated representation of my personality.

It's about recognising that even seemingly insignificant decisions can carry meaning when they're made deliberately.

In the end, I'm still the same developer who enjoys experimenting with technologies, writing about what he learns, and occasionally spending hours trying to understand something he could quite easily have ignored.

The difference is that my portfolio now carries something no library, framework, or design tool could ever have chosen for me.

A part of my history.

A part of my family.

A part of who I am and what I intend to build.

## Appendix — A (Un)Necessary Chromatic and Ideological Clarification

There is, however, one rather unfortunate matter that requires immediate clarification.

It pains me, on an almost spiritual level, to admit that my family's colour is crimson.

Because crimson, despite all its historical, philosophical, and heraldic significance, bears an absolutely scandalous resemblance to red.

And red, as anyone vaguely familiar with Brazilian politics will understand, presents certain ideological complications.

Naturally, I could simply explain that colours do not possess political affiliations, that electromagnetic radiation has no concept of economic policy, and that a hexadecimal value cannot reasonably be held accountable for the ideological crimes committed in its name.

But that would be far too simple.

And, frankly, rather disappointing.

Therefore, I propose a more rigorous examination of the matter.

First, let us consider the visible electromagnetic spectrum.

Visible light occupies approximately the range between 380 and 700 nanometres, although the exact boundaries vary depending on the observer and the definition employed.

Red occupies the longer-wavelength end of this spectrum. Beyond it lies infrared radiation, which is invisible to the human eye.

This is where matters become interesting.

My chosen colour, `#9E1B33`, possesses an HSL hue of approximately 349 degrees, placing it remarkably close to conventional red, whilst introducing a subtle magenta component.

Now, a particularly irritating physicist might point out that crimson, being a colour produced through a combination of RGB values on a screen, does not correspond to a single electromagnetic wavelength.

That same physicist might further insist that crimson is not infrared, cannot be classified as infrared, and that my entire argument demonstrates a worrying misunderstanding of colour science.

All perfectly valid observations.

All entirely beside the point.

For the purposes of this investigation, I propose that the Crimson should be understood not merely as a colour, but as a philosophical movement towards the limits of the visible spectrum.

It is red, certainly, but red subjected to sufficient darkness, complexity, and existential suffering that it begins to contemplate abandoning the visible spectrum altogether.

In other words, it is spiritually infrared.

And infrared, crucially, is not visible.

This distinction is fundamental.

Because if my crimson is symbolically approaching the invisible, then the extent to which it can be associated with the visible red of Brazilian political symbolism becomes increasingly questionable.

We may therefore establish the following propositions:

1. Crimson is not identical to pure red.
2. The Crimson is darker and chromatically more complex than pure red.
3. Infrared exists beyond the visible red region of the electromagnetic spectrum.
4. My crimson, whilst not physically infrared, exhibits an admirable philosophical inclination towards it.
5. Political ideologies cannot be meaningfully derived from wavelengths, RGB values, or CSS custom properties.

The fifth proposition is particularly troublesome, as it renders the preceding four almost entirely unnecessary.

Nevertheless, I have already written them, and I refuse to let perfectly good pseudoscientific reasoning go to waste.

We must also consider the historical problem.

Crimson existed long before modern political parties began appropriating colours for their respective banners, movements, and campaigns.

Consequently, assigning permanent ideological ownership of a colour to any particular political organisation would be historically absurd.

Indeed, if colour alone determined political allegiance, a considerable proportion of European aristocracy, the British military, Ferrari enthusiasts, and several centuries of textile manufacturers would require immediate ideological reassessment.

An administrative nightmare, to say the least.

I therefore submit that the Crimson is historically independent, aesthetically deliberate, scientifically visible, philosophically infrared, and politically unaffiliated.

Having established this through an unnecessarily elaborate argument involving optics, heraldry, political symbolism, and a rather questionable interpretation of the electromagnetic spectrum, I believe we can finally reach a definitive conclusion.

**My colour is crimson. It may look red. It may even, technically, belong to the red family. But let there be absolutely no misunderstanding: I am not a supporter of the Workers' Party (PT), a socialist, a communist, or any other damned '-ist' of that particular ideological family. I never have been, never will be, and shall never subscribe to those particularly 'red' political ideas.**

There.

An entire chromatic dissertation to clarify something a single sentence could have settled.

*Ex Labore, Libertas. Not Ex Rubro, Socialismus.*
